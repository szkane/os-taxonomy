Common misconceptions (from teaching research)

Subtraction as taking away or separatingReports the amount TAKEN AWAY (2) instead of what is left — attends to the action, not the result.Next step: Physically separate the 2, then draw attention to the remaining group and ask 'how many are LEFT'; label result vs action.
Addition as combining or putting together twoNames one visible group as the total — answers 3 (or 2), treating 'altogether' as relabelling a group rather than merging both.Next step: Physically push the groups together and count the single combined set; contrast 'how many red' with 'how many altogether' so combining is felt.
Numbers up to 10 into pairsBelieves a number has ONE correct decomposition — offers only 2+3 and insists that is 'the' way.Next step: Model several splits of the same 5 and record each as an equation; establish that all are correct and the whole is unchanged.
Number bonds to 9Counts up by ones to 10 (or recounts the counters) instead of using the frame's empty cells — treats it as a count, not a complement.Next step: Direct attention to the empty cells as the answer and build the pair-structure via part-part-whole so the complement is seen, not counted.
Fluent adding and subtracting within 5Mishandles zero — gives 3 − 3 as 3, or thinks an operation must change the number.Next step: Act out taking all away (and adding nothing) with objects to establish 0 as a legitimate result.
Addition and subtraction word problemsCombines both numbers whatever the story — adds 6 and 2 for the take-away problem, or applies one default operation.Next step: Act the story out with counters step by step before counting; link it to the take-away representation.

Activity ideas

  • Take-away mat: remove and read what's left
  • Difference mode: 'how many more?' as subtraction
  • Mystery removed: reason back to the missing part
  • Still-scene anchor and take-from stories
