🎯 What you’ll learn

  • 🍕Tenths — Count up and down in tenths; recognise that tenths arise from dividing an object into 10 equal parts and from dividing one-digit numbers or quantities by 10
  • 🍕Fractions on a number line — Recognise and use fractions as numbers: place unit fractions and non-unit fractions with small denominators on a number line
  • 🍕Splitting shapes into equal parts (age 7+) — Partition circles and rectangles into two, three, or four equal shares; describe shares as halves, thirds, and fourths; recognise that equal shares of identical wholes …
  • 🍕Equivalent fractions — Recognise and show, using diagrams, equivalent fractions with small denominators
  • 🍕Simple Fraction Sums — Add and subtract fractions with the same denominator within one whole (e.g. 5/7 + 1/7 = 6/7)
  • 🍕Unit fractions — Recognise, find, and write fractions of a discrete set of objects: unit fractions and non-unit fractions with small denominators
  • 🍕Comparing fractions (age 7+) — Solve problems involving counting in tenths, fractions of quantities, equivalence, fraction addition/subtraction, and fraction comparison
📖 Goals, common misconceptions & printable ideas

Learning goal

Your child is discovering fractions as parts of a whole — understanding halves, thirds, and quarters, placing fractions on number lines, and beginning to add and subtract simple fractions.

Common misconceptions (from teaching research)

TenthsWhole-number bias — ten-tenths read as 'ten', and a tenth judged bigger than a half 'because 10 is more than 2'Next step: Recompose ten tenths into one whole in front of the child; overlay five tenths on one half; reteach the denominator as parts-of-one-whole
Fractions on a number lineRules-the-line like a ruler — counts tick marks (or draws 4 marks for fourths) instead of equal segments, so the point is misread or the unit is mis-partitionedNext step: Show fourths as four equal SEGMENTS made by THREE marks between 0 and 1; have the child colour each segment and count segments, not marks (IES Roadblock 2.1)
Splitting shapes into equal parts (age 7+)Equal shares must look the same — rejects equal-area but different-shape pieces as not being quartersNext step: Rearrange/overlay the different-shaped pieces so they tile onto one another; count unit squares under each; establish equal AMOUNT as the test
Equivalent fractionsWhole-number bias — the fraction with bigger numbers/more pieces is judged to be more (4/6 > 2/3 because 4 > 2 and 6 > 3)Next step: Overlay the two identical bars so the shaded lengths coincide; split each third to generate the sixths in front of the child so the amount is seen not to change
Simple Fraction SumsAdds/subtracts the denominators as well: '2 and 2 is 4 on top, 5 and 5 is 10 on the bottom, so 4/10' — operates on the denominator as if it were a second whole number.Next step: Return to a fixed-unit strip: 2/5 and 2/5 are four SAME-SIZE fifth-pieces, so the answer is fifths; the fifth (denominator) names the piece and can't change when you just count more of them. Contrast tenths physically not fitting the fifths bar.
Unit fractionsTakes the denominator as the answer — grabs 4 counters for 1/4 'because there's a 4 in the fraction'Next step: Share the 12 into 4 equal groups on the tray; one group (3 counters) is 1/4. The 4 counts the GROUPS, not the objects.

Try at home (predict–observe–explain)

Show the child a whole strip. Ask: 'If I cut this into ten equal pieces and then lay all ten back end-to-end in a row, will the row be shorter than, the same length as, or LONGER than the whole strip? …

Activity ideas

  • Collect the tenths back into one whole
  • Share three wholes among ten to see 3/10 = 3÷10
  • Equal parts only, and the same whole
  • Place and name tenths on a number line
