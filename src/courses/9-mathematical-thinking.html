📖 Goals, common misconceptions & printable ideas

Learning goal

Your child is developing advanced problem-solving skills, learning to choose the best mathematical tools and methods for complex problems, and communicating their mathematical reasoning clearly.

Common misconceptions (from teaching research)

Complex Multi-Step ProblemsWeak magnitude sense with percentages/decimals — accepts a percentage over 100% (or a part larger than the whole) as a valid answer in context.Next step: Anchor percentages to a bar/double number line where 100% is the whole group; revisit percentage–decimal–fraction equivalents so magnitude is felt.
Precise Maths VocabularyConfuses factor and multiple — uses them interchangeably or in the wrong direction ('6 is a multiple of 12')Next step: Use arrays and factor pairs — a factor fits into the number, a multiple is a step in its times-table — and rephrase loose usage into the precise term. Reteach in place.
Real-World Maths ModellingReports the discount as the sale price ('it's £10') — computes the change but not the resulting quantity the question asked forNext step: Draw the percentage bar and shade the discount vs the part left: 'you found the bit taken OFF; the price is the bit that STAYS'. Route the answer back through 'what does the shopper hand over?'
Understanding fractions (age 9+)Empirical proof scheme: treats confirming examples or a careful drawing as proof — 'I tried it and it worked, so it's true.'Next step: Confront the asymmetry: 'How many pizzas would you have to try to be SURE? Could there ever be one where it's different?' Then co-build the common-denominator reason and name it as the kind of argument that covers every case at once.
Choosing representations strategicallyUses common denominators for every comparison, even 3/8 vs 1/2, never reaching for a benchmarkNext step: Teach the 'compare to a half first' check and reserve the full algorithm for when benchmarks cannot separate — a self-regulation issue, reteach in place.
Fractions on a number line (age 9+)Fraction, decimal and percentage treated as three different things, not one magnitude — picks one as 'the' right label or can't relate them.Next step: Use the single hundredths grid / one number line with all three notations on the same region until equivalence is a fact of magnitude; reteach fraction–decimal–percentage as the same quantity.
