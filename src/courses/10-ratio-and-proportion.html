🎯 What you’ll learn

  • 🥤Scale and similar shapes — Solve problems involving similar shapes where the scale factor is known or can be found
  • 🥤Calculating Percentages — Solve problems involving the calculation of percentages of amounts (e.g. 15% of 360) and the use of percentages for comparison
  • 🥤Ratio Problems — Solve problems involving the relative sizes of two quantities where missing values can be found by using integer multiplication and division facts
  • 🥤Understanding fractions — Solve problems involving unequal sharing and grouping using knowledge of fractions and multiples
📖 Goals, common misconceptions & printable ideas

Learning goal

Your child is learning to compare quantities and solve problems involving ratios, proportions, percentages, and scale — essential skills for understanding relationships between numbers in real-world contexts.

Common misconceptions (from teaching research)

Scale and similar shapesAdditive enlargement — adds the constant difference (here +12) to each side instead of multiplying by the scale factorNext step: Ask 'is the new rectangle the SAME shape as the old one?' Draw both and overlay — the additive version is distorted. Rebuild on a ratio table: every side ×3. Reteach multiplicative missing-value ratio.
Calculating PercentagesCompares the percentages and ignores the different bases: 'the jacket, because 30% is more than 25%' — without working out the actual savingsNext step: Force 'percent of WHAT?' — compute both actual amounts on two labelled bars of different heights; show the smaller percentage taking the bigger slice. Reinforce percentage as relative to its own base.
Ratio ProblemsAdditive / constant-difference reasoning: 'oranges went up by 15, so apples go up by 15' (→ 17), or 'the difference stays 3' — uses a constant difference instead of a constant multiplierNext step: Confront additively vs multiplicatively on a perceptible mixture: show 'add the difference' makes a different mixture, while scaling keeps it. Rebuild via 'what do we multiply 5 by to get 20?' and a bar model. This is documented additive-strategy interference, not carelessness.
Understanding fractionsReads 3:5 as the fraction 3/5 of the whole — uses one part as the denominator instead of the sum of partsNext step: Draw the bar cut into 3+5 = 8 equal segments; count them. The denominator is the TOTAL parts, so it's 3/8. Contrast 3/8 with 3/5 explicitly. Reteach part-to-part vs part-to-whole vocabulary.

Activity ideas

  • Enlargement studio: grab, scale, and compare with additive mode
  • Confront the additive error on paper
  • Solve and judge similar-shape problems
  • Optional extension: scaling a solid to feel k² and k³
