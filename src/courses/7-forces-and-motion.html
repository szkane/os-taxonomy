🎯 What you’ll learn

  • 🏎️Contact & Non-Contact Forces — Notice that some forces need contact between two objects (contact forces) while magnetic forces can act at a distance (non-contact forces)
  • 🏎️Magnetic Materials — Observe how magnets attract or repel each other and attract some materials and not others
  • 🏎️Magnetic Poles — Describe magnets as having two poles (north and south) and predict whether two magnets will attract or repel based on which poles face each other
📖 Goals, common misconceptions & printable ideas

Learning goal

Your child is exploring forces around them — discovering how magnets work, understanding that some forces need contact while others work at a distance, and investigating how different surfaces affect how things move.

Common misconceptions (from teaching research)

Contact & Non-Contact Forces'They must really be touching somehow — there's something invisible in the gap joining them.'Next step: Lift the paperclip toward the magnet through a sheet of paper or clear plastic — nothing spans the gap yet the force acts. Name it non-contact.
Magnetic Materials'All metals are magnetic — the magnet will pull all three because they're metal.'Next step: Test copper, aluminium and brass as counter-examples in a predict-test-record-conclude cycle; name iron, steel, nickel and cobalt as the magnetic ones.
Magnetic Poles'They'll snap together — magnets always attract each other.'Next step: Give plentiful like-pole trials so the child FEELS the repulsion; label the poles and state the rule.

Try at home (predict–observe–explain)

Set a paperclip a small gap from a magnet. Ask the child to predict whether the pull still works (a) with a sheet of paper slipped into the gap and (b) with a steel baking tray slipped in — and WHY. …

Activity ideas

  • Feel and see the pull across an empty gap
  • Predict-then-test: what gets through the gap?
  • Distinguish gravity from magnetism
  • Sort everyday forces and explain the rule
