🎯 What you’ll learn

  • 🧠Making Sense of Problems — Make sense of a problem by identifying what is being asked, choosing concrete objects or pictures to represent the situation, and explaining a pathway to the solution
  • 🧠Using objects to model real problems — Use objects, drawings, or simple number sentences to represent a real-world situation (early mathematical modelling)
  • 🧠Spotting mathematical patterns — Notice simple patterns and structures: spot that changing order doesn't change the total, and recognise how numbers relate to each other
  • 🧠Real-World to Maths Connections — Move between a real-world situation and a mathematical representation using concrete objects, drawings, diagrams, tables, number sentences, or bar models
  • 🧠Finding efficient methods — Notice when a calculation or pattern repeats and use this to count more efficiently or predict results
  • 🧠Hands-On Problem Solving — Select and use familiar tools (concrete objects, fingers, ten frames) to help solve a mathematical problem
📖 Goals, common misconceptions & printable ideas

Learning goal

Your child is beginning to think like a mathematician — using objects and pictures to solve problems, explaining their reasoning, noticing patterns, and connecting maths to real-world situations.

Common misconceptions (from teaching research)

Using objects to model real problemsModels the numbers but not the action: accepts or draws 5 and 2 more (adding) when the story is taking away — maps the digits, not what happened.Next step: Act the eating out with real objects so the taking-away is physical; reteach representing subtraction word problems with objects/drawings that show the action.
Spotting mathematical patternsOver-extends 'order doesn't matter' to subtraction, saying 5 take away 3 is the same as 3 take away 5Next step: Act out a subtraction story with objects (you can't take 5 sweets from a pile of 3) so the child sees the swap fails here; name that the pattern holds for adding, not taking away
Real-World to Maths ConnectionsTreats the model as two separate amounts, not a relationship: builds 5 and 2 but cannot identify the whole or show that joining them makes it — sees a picture, not a part-part-whole.Next step: Rebuild the part-whole physically: push the two groups together under one drawn bar and ask 'what's the same, what's different?'; reteach representing the situation with objects so the relationship, not just the amounts, is modelled.
Hands-On Problem SolvingReaches for the same familiar tool every time by comfort, not fit — 'I always use my fingers' — even when the numbers cross tenNext step: Pose paired problems (3+2 then 8+7) where the same tool fits one and fails the other, and make the finger limitation visible; ask which tool the numbers call for.

Activity ideas

  • The thinking partner who won't take a number yet
  • Numberless, progressively-revealed story with a part-whole workspace
  • Tap-to-reveal worked example of the four moves
  • Single reasonableness check (which answer could not be right?)
