🎯 What you’ll learn

  • ⬡Position, direction, and movement — Use mathematical vocabulary to describe position, direction, and movement, including straight lines and distinguishing rotation as a turn in terms of right angles …
  • ⬡2-D shapes (age 6+) — Identify and describe properties of 2-D shapes including the number of sides and line symmetry in a vertical line
  • ⬡Defining attributes of shapes — Distinguish defining attributes of shapes (e.g. triangles are closed and three-sided) from non-defining attributes (e.g. colour, orientation, overall size)
  • ⬡Understanding angles — Recognise angles as a property of shape or a description of a turn
  • ⬡Edges, vertices, and faces — Identify and describe properties of 3-D shapes including the number of edges, vertices, and faces
  • ⬡Sorting 2-D and 3-D shapes — Compare and sort common 2-D and 3-D shapes and everyday objects by their properties
  • ⬡Lines of symmetry — Complete a simple symmetric figure with respect to a specific line of symmetry
  • ⬡Building with 3-D Shapes — Compose three-dimensional shapes (cubes, right rectangular prisms, right circular cones, right circular cylinders) and create composite shapes; build new shapes from …
  • ⬡Composing Shapes — Compose two-dimensional shapes (rectangles, squares, trapezoids, triangles, half-circles, quarter-circles) to create composite shapes, and compose new shapes from the …
  • ⬡2-D faces on 3-D shapes — Identify 2-D shapes on the surface of 3-D shapes (e.g. a circle on a cylinder, a triangle on a pyramid)
📖 Goals, common misconceptions & printable ideas

Learning goal

Your child is discovering the world of shapes — identifying properties of 2D and 3D shapes, learning how to combine shapes to make new ones, and describing positions and movements using mathematical language.

Common misconceptions (from teaching research)

Position, direction, and movementTurns anti-clockwise when told clockwise (or treats the two as the same) — the new convention not yet stable ('clockwise... this way?')Next step: Return to a real clock's hand direction as the anchor and label both ways explicitly each time.
2-D shapes (age 6+)Believes any line that divides a shape into two equal/congruent parts is a line of symmetry — accepts the rectangle's diagonal 'because it makes two triangles the same'.Next step: Cut out the rectangle and fold along the diagonal — the halves do not land on each other, a corner overhangs; contrast with folding along the vertical midline where they coincide exactly. Establish: a line of symmetry is a fold where the halves MATCH, not just where the pieces are the same size.
Defining attributes of shapesTreats orientation as defining (an 'imposed attribute'): a rotated square is a 'diamond'; a triangle 'must' sit on a horizontal baseNext step: Rotate the SAME cut-out slowly — 'is it still a square now? and now?' — so the child sees the four equal sides and square corners do not change as it turns; name orientation as a 'doesn't-matter'.
Understanding anglesThe angle with the longer lines is bigger ('these lines are longer, so it's a bigger angle')Next step: Overlay the two angles vertex-on-vertex with one arm aligned so the openings coincide; show the arms differ in length but the opening is identical. Return to the hinge model, lengthening the arms with the opening held fixed.
Edges, vertices, and facesBlurs the three properties, using 'side' or 'corner' loosely so face, edge and vertex run together when counting.Next step: Count by touch in a fixed order — colour each face, trace each edge, dot each vertex — naming the property each time, so the three stay distinct.
Sorting 2-D and 3-D shapesSorts by a non-defining feature — colour, size or 'the pretty ones' — rather than a geometric property.Next step: Restrict the collection to a single colour and size so appearance cannot be the criterion, forcing attention to a property; model a property rule aloud ('I'm putting all the ones with a curved surface here').

Try at home (predict–observe–explain)

Hold up a cut-out non-square rectangle. 'Predict: if we fold this rectangle exactly along its diagonal, corner to corner, will the two halves land exactly on top of each other? Say yes or no before we fold.' A child who equates congruence with symmetry predicts YES (two identical triangles). …

Activity ideas

  • Clockwise, anti-clockwise and the right angle a quarter turn leaves
  • Give and follow a multi-step route that someone else can act on
  • Check and sort turn and route language
  • Fold test: does each half land on the other?
