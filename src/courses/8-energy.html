🎯 What you’ll learn

  • 🔋Building a simple circuit — Construct a simple series electrical circuit, identifying and naming its basic parts: cells, wires, bulbs, switches, and buzzers
  • 🔋What uses electricity at home — Identify common appliances that run on electricity and understand that electricity is a form of energy that powers devices in everyday life
  • 🔋How energy travels around — Observe and provide evidence that energy can be transferred from place to place by sound, light, heat, and electric currents
  • 🔋Heat sources and temperature change — Identify sources of heat energy (sun, fire, heaters, friction, bodies) and examine how the temperatures of two objects change as heat energy moves from the hotter to the …
  • 🔋Reading and drawing circuit diagrams — Draw and read simple circuit diagrams using standard symbols for cells, bulbs, switches, buzzers, and wires; identify whether a circuit is complete or broken from a …
  • 🔋How switches work — Recognise that a switch opens and closes a circuit, controlling whether a lamp lights or a buzzer sounds
  • 🔋Will the bulb light up? — Identify whether or not a lamp will light in a simple series circuit, based on whether the lamp is part of a complete loop with a battery
  • 🔋Conductors and insulators — Recognise some common conductors and insulators, and associate metals with being good conductors of electricity
📖 Goals, common misconceptions & printable ideas

Learning goal

Your child is discovering how electricity works — building simple circuits with batteries and bulbs, understanding how switches control electrical flow, and learning which materials conduct electricity and which don't.

Common misconceptions (from teaching research)

Building a simple circuitOne wire is enough — runs a single wire from one battery terminal to the bulb and expects it to light ('electricity flows out to the bulb and that's it') — the unipolar model.Next step: Highlight that the current must RETURN to the other terminal — trace the loop with a finger and connect the second wire.
What uses electricity at homeAnything with a plug or that you switch on uses electricity, and anything without a plug doesn't ('no plug, no electricity').Next step: Sort by ENERGY SOURCE, not by plug — mains, battery, wind-up/other; show that a battery is a portable store of electrical energy.
How energy travels aroundThe battery sends electricity out to the bulb where it's used up, so less (or nothing) comes back — the consumption model.Next step: It's ENERGY that's transferred at the bulb, not the charge — an ammeter reads the same before and after the bulb; the charge goes round and back.
Reading and drawing circuit diagramsDraws little pictures — a battery drawing, a light-bulb picture — instead of the standard symbols ('I'll just draw what it looks like').Next step: Match each real component to its agreed symbol on a key card, then redraw using only symbols.
How switches workThe switch turns the electricity on and off like a tap, or makes the power ('the switch gives the bulb its electricity').Next step: Show that the cell is the energy source; the switch only closes or opens the loop — move the switch to different places and it still just breaks the loop.
Will the bulb light up?It lights as long as a wire reaches the bulb from the battery — one path is enough ('electricity goes to the bulb') — the unipolar model.Next step: Trace for a RETURN path to the other terminal; no loop, no light.

Try at home (predict–observe–explain)

Put an ammeter in the circuit just before the bulb, then just after it. Ask the child to predict whether the reading after the bulb will be smaller (because the bulb 'used some up'), the same, or larger — AND why. …
