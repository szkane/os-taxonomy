Common misconceptions (from teaching research)

Probability as a FractionWrites the probability as a part-part ratio/odds (favourable to unfavourable) instead of the part-whole fraction — e.g. 3/2 or 3:2 for 3 red among 5Next step: Recount the WHOLE set aloud including the reds; the denominator is every counter that could be drawn, so the total (5), not the leftovers (2), goes on the bottom
Experimental vs TheoreticalExpects experimental results to equal the theoretical value and concludes bias from a short runNext step: Do not reteach the mechanics — extend the run (or pool data) so the relative frequency settles nearer 1/6; a deviation over few trials is expected, bias needs many trials to judge
Ordering LikelihoodsCompares by absolute count of favourable outcomes — 'Bag B, it has more red counters (6 beats 3)'Next step: Direct attention to the whole: cover the blues and ask 'out of ALL the counters, how much of each bag is red?'; shade three-quarters of A and one-half of B side by side
Equally Likely OutcomesNumber of outcome types implies equal likelihood — 'both spinners are fair because each has three colours'Next step: Overlay the sections: red on Y covers as much as blue and green together, so it cannot have the same chance; ask what would make Y fair (three equal sections)

Try at home (predict–observe–explain)

Show a spinner divided so that red fills three-quarters and blue one-quarter, alongside a fair coin. Ask: 'Over 20 spins, will red and blue come up about the same number of times, or will one come up much more — and why?' Have the child state a prediction AND a reason before spinning. …

Activity ideas

  • Interpret a given fraction: what does each number count?
  • Construct and translate from a fresh situation
  • Check the outcomes are equally likely before counting
