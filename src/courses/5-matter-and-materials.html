🎯 What you’ll learn

  • 🧊States of Matter Vocabulary — Name and distinguish the three states of matter — solid, liquid, and gas — using properties vocabulary: hard, rigid, runny, flows, keeps its shape, fills its container; …
  • 🧊Describing Material Properties — Describe simple physical properties of everyday materials such as hard/soft, stretchy/stiff, shiny/dull, rough/smooth, waterproof/absorbent, transparent/opaque
  • 🧊Objects vs Materials — Distinguish between an object and the material from which it is made, understanding that objects can be made from different materials
  • 🧊Naming Everyday Materials — Identify and name a variety of everyday materials including wood, plastic, glass, metal, water, and rock
  • 🧊Grouping Materials — Compare and group everyday materials based on their simple physical properties
  • 🧊Choosing the Right Material — Identify and compare the suitability of everyday materials for particular uses, explaining why specific materials are chosen for specific purposes
📖 Goals, common misconceptions & printable ideas

Learning goal

Your child is exploring the materials around them — identifying different substances like wood, plastic, and metal, and discovering their properties such as whether they're hard or soft, rough or smooth.

Common misconceptions (from teaching research)

States of Matter VocabularyAir/gas is 'nothing' — won't call the air in the balloon a gas or a material; treats gas as empty space.Next step: Make gas tangible: squeeze the sealed balloon, fan the air onto a face, feel it resist — a gas fills its container and can be felt, so it is a real material.
Describing Material PropertiesJudges a property from appearance rather than testing it — 'it looks smooth and shiny so it must be waterproof'.Next step: Introduce a simple fair test (drip the same amount of water on each, watch for soak-through) so a property becomes something observed, not guessed; let the surprise result do the teaching.
Objects vs MaterialsObject and material are the same thing — the child answers 'what is the object?' with the stuff it's made of ('it's the metal one', 'it's wood') and has no separate word for the thing versus the stuff.Next step: Confront directly: hold up two DIFFERENT objects made of the SAME material (wooden spoon + wooden block) — 'same stuff, different things' — then two same objects of different material, naming object then material each time.
Naming Everyday MaterialsWater isn't a material — 'materials' must be solid (and usually man-made), so a liquid doesn't count.Next step: State the rule: a material is what something is made of, in any state — the sea, a puddle and ice are all the material water; connect to States of Matter vocabulary.
Grouping MaterialsSorts by object type or use rather than material property — 'the cup and the jar go together because they hold things'.Next step: Redirect to 'what is it MADE of / what is it LIKE to touch?'; if it persists, reteach the property-describing step before grouping.
Choosing the Right MaterialJustifies by familiarity/convention, not property — 'windows are glass because windows are always glass'.Next step: Redirect to function: 'what does this object need to DO?' — list the jobs, then ask which property meets each; the choice follows from the property.

Try at home (predict–observe–explain)

Hold up a shiny paper towel and a dull plastic bag. Before pouring anything, ask the child to PREDICT which will keep a small toy dry and to say WHY. Record the reason. Then pour the same splash of water over each: the 'smart-looking' shiny paper soaks through, the dull plastic stays dry. …

Activity ideas

  • Tap-to-name the three states in a kitchen scene
  • Sort real substances into solid / liquid / gas bins
  • Use the words like a scientist (checkpoint)
  • Materials test bench: watch the property happen
