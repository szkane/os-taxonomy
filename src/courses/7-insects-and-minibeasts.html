Common misconceptions (from teaching research)

The insect body planAttaches legs to the abdomen or spreads them along the whole body, rather than all six to the thoraxNext step: Look at a real beetle or a large clear photo and trace each leg back to where it joins — all to the middle. Name the thorax as 'the part the legs and wings come from'.
Not all minibeasts are insectsThinks any small crawling creature is an insect — 'spiders, worms, woodlice are all insects, they're all bugs'Next step: Make the six-legs rule the sole gate: count legs before sorting. Spider = 8 → out. Let the count overrule the 'it's a bug' feeling.
Sorting and Identifying MinibeastsAnswers a key question wrongly because they miscount or mis-observe the feature (e.g. counts a spider as 6 legs)Next step: Slow down the observation step: count and touch each leg before answering. The key only works if the feature is read correctly — reteach the insect/non-insect features if needed.
Social insects: ants and beesThinks the queen is a ruler who gives orders and makes decisions ('she's the boss and tells them what to do')Next step: Reframe 'queen' as a job title, not a throne: her job is laying eggs. The colony runs on shared instinctive roles, not orders. Compare to a team where everyone knows their task without a boss shouting.
Bees and pollinationThinks the bee only takes from the flower and gives nothing back ('it's just getting food')Next step: Show pollen sticking to a fuzzy bee (dust a pipe-cleaner 'bee' in flour and touch a second 'flower'). Make the accidental transfer visible — the bee carries pollen without meaning to.
Camouflage, warning colours, and mimicryThinks bright warning colours are for decoration or to attract mates/friends, not to warn predators awayNext step: Frame colour as a message to predators: 'stay away, I'll hurt you'. Ask what a bird learns after being stung once — it avoids the pattern next time. Bright can mean danger, not beauty.
