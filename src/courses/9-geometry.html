🎯 What you’ll learn

  • ⬡Degrees and turns — Know that angles are measured in degrees, where one degree is 1/360 of a full turn; understand that an angle turning through n one-degree angles has a measure of n …
  • ⬡What Is an Angle? — Understand that an angle is a geometric shape formed by two rays sharing a common endpoint (vertex); recognise angles in real-life contexts and 2-D shapes
  • ⬡Straight, reflex and revolution angles — Name, estimate and compare straight angles, reflex angles and revolutions alongside acute, right and obtuse angles, describing each by its relationship to a right angle …
  • ⬡Lines, Rays & Angles — Draw and identify points, lines, line segments, rays, angles (right, acute, obtuse), and perpendicular and parallel lines; identify these in two-dimensional figures
  • ⬡Measuring angles — Measure angles in whole-number degrees using a protractor; draw given angles and sketch angles of specified measure
  • ⬡Regular and irregular polygons — Distinguish between regular and irregular polygons based on reasoning about equal sides and equal angles
  • ⬡Classifying shapes by line properties — Classify two-dimensional figures based on the presence or absence of parallel or perpendicular lines, or angles of a specified size; recognise right triangles as a …
  • ⬡Angle Sum Rules — Know that angles at a point sum to 360° (one whole turn), angles on a straight line sum to 180°, and vertically opposite angles are equal; use these facts to find …
  • ⬡Estimating Angles — Estimate and compare acute, obtuse, and reflex angles in degrees; classify angles by type and order them by size
  • ⬡Understanding angles (age 9+) — Use the properties of rectangles to deduce related facts and find missing lengths and angles
  • ⬡Transformations on a Grid — Identify, describe, and represent the position of a shape following a reflection or translation using appropriate language; know that the shape has not changed
  • ⬡3-D shapes (age 9+) — Identify 3-D shapes, including cubes and other cuboids, from 2-D representations
  • ⬡Rotational symmetry — Recognise rotational symmetry in shapes and designs, state how many times a shape matches itself in a full turn, and create patterns with line and rotational symmetry, …
  • ⬡Approximating objects with shapes — Represent and approximate composite shapes and objects in the environment using combinations of familiar 2-D shapes and 3-D objects
📖 Goals, common misconceptions & printable ideas

Learning goal

Your child is learning to measure and work with angles using protractors, classify shapes by their properties, and understand transformations like reflections and translations while exploring how angles relate to each other.

Common misconceptions (from teaching research)

Degrees and turnsThe long-armed angle is more degrees ('bigger lines, more degrees'), or a degree measures length / arc rather than turnNext step: Rotate a ray to build the angle and show the degree count is the amount of turn, independent of arm length.
What Is an Angle?Treats a turn, a corner and an opening as different things rather than one general angle ('that's a corner, not an angle', or 'a door opening isn't an angle')Next step: Lay the different situations side by side and strip each to a vertex and two arms, naming the shared structure they all have.
Lines, Rays & Angles'The angle with the longer arms (or wider-apart ends) is the bigger angle' — judges angle size by ray length or endpoint gap, not the opening at the vertexNext step: Overlay the two angles vertex-on-vertex so the arms coincide, then extend/shorten one arm live to show the opening is unchanged; reground angle as amount of turn using a fan/wedge before re-testing
Measuring anglesReads the wrong scale — reports the mirror value ('it's 133' for a clearly acute 47° angle) because they read whichever number the ray crosses without checking acute/obtuse.Next step: Reinstate estimate-first: before every read, commit to acute or obtuse, then use that to pick the scale; check the read against a 90° benchmark. Reteach the degree-size sense if the acute/obtuse judgement itself is shaky.
Regular and irregular polygonsRegular = equal sides only — 'yes, it's regular, all four sides are the same', ignoring that the angles are not equalNext step: Add the angle check explicitly — run BOTH conditions on the rhombus so the child sees it passes sides but fails angles; contrast with a regular pentagon and with the square. Reteach in place: applying the second criterion IS the target.
Classifying shapes by line propertiesConfuses the vocabulary — swaps 'parallel' and 'perpendicular', e.g. calls a right-angle corner 'parallel' or a pair of parallel sides 'perpendicular'Next step: Re-anchor each word to its check and image: parallel lines run alongside and never meet (slide the ruler, the gap holds); perpendicular lines cross at a right angle (set-square clicks). Rehearse naming while checking.

Try at home (predict–observe–explain)

Show a right angle drawn with short arms. Ask the child to predict how many degrees it becomes if you extend both arms much longer, and why, before extending. Then extend the arms (or overlay a long-armed right angle, vertex aligned). …

Activity ideas

  • Sweep a ray and count degrees of turn
  • Partition a full turn and name the one-degree angle
  • Build and combine benchmark angles with pattern blocks
  • Check understanding with varied response formats
